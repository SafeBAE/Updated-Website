# Supporting a Survivor

Healing after sexual violence is deeply personal, with no single path forward. Many survivors find strength in connection — whether with peers, supportive communities, or professionals. These relationships can ease feelings of isolation, reduce guilt or shame, and provide tools for coping. Even when survivors understand they are not to blame, it often takes time and support to fully believe it.

For some, reconnecting with their body and emotions may feel overwhelming. Some survivors experience disconnection or turn to risky behaviors to manage pain. While facing these feelings can be difficult, avoiding them can cause further harm. With compassionate support and mental health resources, survivors can begin to process trauma, reclaim safety, and move toward healing.

Relationships and intimacy after assault can bring new challenges. Survivors may face triggers that complicate dating, while partners may feel uncertain about how to provide support. To open space for these conversations, SafeBAE partnered with *Teen Vogue* to produce [***Dating After Sexual Assault*.**](https://www.safebae.org/resources/videos/dating-a-survivor/) The short video and teaching guide affirm that healing looks different for everyone and offer practical tools for navigating trust, communication, and boundaries, creating space for survivors and partners to explore healthy intimacy after trauma.

## When Someone Tells You They’ve Been Assaulted

**Opening up about sexual violence takes tremendous courage. If someone chooses to share their experience with you, here’s how to show up for them:**

* **Start by believing.** The single best way to support a survivor is to believe them. Questioning their account — even out of shock — can be devastating and may keep them from ever opening up again.
* **Listen more than you speak.** Show you’re engaged: make eye contact, don’t interrupt, and let them share as much or as little as they want. You don’t need details to be helpful — never press for them.
* **Validate their feelings.** If they minimize what happened (“other people have it worse”), gently remind them they’re allowed to be as upset as they need to be. No assault is “not bad enough” to matter.
* **Meet immediate needs first.** Before processing emotions, make sure they feel physically safe and cared for. Something as simple as a glass of water, a snack, a cozy blanket, or asking if they want a hug can signal to their brain that they’re safe now.
* **Let them lead.** Follow their choices about whether to report, who to tell, and how to cope. Reporting can be re-traumatizing, so never pressure them to. You can help them understand their options while respecting whatever they decide.

Try Saying:
“I’m so sorry this happened to you.”
“You are not alone. I know there are people that can help.”
“How can I support you?”
“It’s not your fault.”
“I’m glad you felt safe to tell me so I can be here for you.”
“You didn’t deserve that to happen to you.”

Don’t Say:
“Why didn’t you just leave?”
“Why were you there?”
“If you kissed, you should have known something was going to happen.”
