## A. Choking Hazard (campaign) — source: https://chokinghazard.org (a SafeBAE project)
Site sections: Home, Learn More (/learn-more/), Media Ratings (/media-ratings/), Resources (/resources/), Short Film (/film/). Contact: info@safebae.org.

Hero copy (verbatim): "Prevention has to start early, before the behavior does." / "By college, many young people have already heard about "choking" or encountered it in sexual contexts." / "Waiting until after high school to talk about the impact and harm of sexual strangulation is too late." / "Prevention needs to start earlier."

Statistics as the site reports them (keep the citation):
- 2022 national U.S. survey: 11.9% of sexually experienced girls aged 14–17 said a partner had choked them; 31.9% of women 18–24; 40.6% of women 25–29. (Herbenick et al.)
- Average age students first heard about choking during sex: 15. Most common first sources: friends (35%) and pornography (34.5%). (Herbenick et al., 2025)
- About 1 in 4 college students who had been choked were first choked between ages 10 and 17. (Herbenick et al., 2022)
- Carotid arteries close at about 11 lb of force; opening a can takes about 20 lb. These are not safe limits. (Armstrong & Strack, 2016)
- A person can pass out within 5–10 seconds (6.8 seconds on average in one study). Breathing can stop by about 62 seconds. (Rossen et al., 1943; Sauvageau et al., 2011)
- 2025 Australian survey of 4,702 adults aged 18–35: 79.1% of people who choked a partner said the partner actively agreed, vs 56.6% of people who were choked. (Sharman, Fitzgerald & Douglas, 2025)
- 1 in 2 U.S. college students choked during their most recent sex had no agreed word or signal to stop; 38% relied on body language alone. (Herbenick et al., 2025)
- 316 choking memes analysed: common themes were confusion about communication and consent, and jokes about injury or death. (Herbenick et al., 2023)
- U.S. print romance sales more than doubled in four years, to 51 million copies a year. (Circana BookScan, 2025)
- Archive of Our Own lists more than 82,000 works under its "Choking" tag. (checked September 2026)
- In a study of 4,254 university students, pornography was one of the most commonly reported places people first saw sexual choking.
- In a review of 300 cases, half had no visible neck injury and 35% had marks too small to photograph.
- Women strangled by an abusive partner had 7.48 times the odds of later being killed by that partner. (Glass et al., 2008)

Myth vs fact (verbatim headings):
- Myth "It's just choking." → Fact "It's strangulation."
- Myth "It's safe if you do it right." → Fact "There's no safe way."
- Myth "No marks, no harm." → Fact "No marks means no marks."
- Myth "They said yes, so it's consent." → Fact "You're still responsible for the harm." Detail: "Strangulation is a crime in every US state. All 50 states, Washington, D.C., and three US territories have added strangulation or suffocation to their criminal codes, most of them under aggravated assault. If a partner is accidentally hurt or killed, the person who choked them may be held legally and criminally responsible, even when both agreed to it. Laws differ from state to state."
- Myth "They felt fine after, so they're fine." → Fact "Some of it shows up later."

Consent (verbatim): "The person doing it is more likely to think it was consensual." … "If you're the one choking someone, don't assume they agreed." "Choking can't meet the pillars of consent." "Even if two people agree to it beforehand, the pillars of consent can't be met once the choking starts."
- Sober: "Consent needs a clear head." "Strangulation cuts off blood to the brain. The person being choked stops thinking clearly before they pass out."
- Revocable: "Consent means you can stop it at any time." "In experiments, some people being strangled could not speak or move to say stop, and some forgot they could end it."
- Ongoing: "Consent has to last the whole time." "A person can pass out 5 to 10 seconds after the carotid arteries close. Someone who is unconscious can't agree to anything."
- Enthusiastic: "Consent has to be clear and keep being shown." "You can't tell by looking whether someone is enjoying it or passing out."

If it has already happened (/resources/): Go to an ER today, even with no marks and even if you agreed to it, if you: passed out (even briefly) or can't remember part of what happened; have breathing changes, or pain or difficulty swallowing; a hoarse, raspy or deeper voice, or trouble speaking; neck pain or tenderness, or a mark or bruise on your neck; vision changes, ringing ears, or hearing changes; a headache, dizziness or lightheadedness; small red spots in your eyes or on your face; lost bladder or bowel control. Call 911 if someone is unconscious, has trouble breathing, has a swelling neck, seems confused, has one-sided weakness or numbness, has a seizure, or shows stroke signs. Symptoms can show up later: throat swelling can develop hours later; a torn-artery stroke can occur days or weeks later. At the front desk say "I was strangled" (not just "my throat hurts"). Under 18: you can get medical care regardless; staff may be required to report abuse of a minor and you can ask what would be reported before sharing more.

If it happened to a friend (verbatim): intro "What you say first affects whether your friend tells anyone else and whether they get medical care." Say: "I'm really sorry that happened to you." / "Are you hurt? Do you want me to go with you to get checked?" / "What can I do to help?" Don't say: "Why didn't you stop it?" / "Lots of people are into that." / "You seem fine, so it probably wasn't a big deal."

Worried you hurt someone (verbatim): "If you strangled someone without their consent, pressured them, or went further than they agreed to, Vibe Check can help you reflect on what happened and what taking responsibility could look like." Link https://checkyourvibe.org

## C. Tattoos & Healing (subpage of #ForDaisy)
SafeBAE source: crawl/md/fordaisy.md — "Daisy was more than her advocacy. She was a tattoo artist whose work people still carry with them, and she was loved deeply by her family and friends." Quote tributes verbatim; skip the one that mentions being suicidal.

Research and reporting (cite each):
- Maxwell, D., Leat, S., Thomas, J., Thomas, S., & Coad, S. (2023). "The tattoo environment as a therapeutic healing space for sexual assault survivors." Deviant Behavior. doi:10.1080/01639625.2023.2250052. Secondary analysis of qualitative interviews with 10 survivors of sexual trauma and 7 tattoo artists. Findings: the studio's look and feel helped it feel comfortable and safe; survivors felt belonging through rapport with the artist and the ritual of getting tattooed; the setting supported clients' autonomy. The authors argue tattoo spaces can work as trauma-informed healing environments.
- Branco, P. (2017, April 3). "Reclaiming the body after trauma: Can body art help abuse survivors heal?" VAWnet / National Resource Center on Domestic Violence. https://vawnet.org/news/reclaiming-body-after-trauma-can-body-art-help-abuse-survivors-heal — research on tattooing and healing from gender-based violence is limited; some studies suggest body art can be restorative; tattoos can also be painful reminders (an abuser's name, a trafficking brand), and cover-ups or removal can help; body art is one option among many and not right for everyone.
- Fletcher, A. A. (2026, March 2). "Reclaiming the Body After Trauma." Psychology Today. https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/keeping-it-real-and-resilient/202603/reclaiming-the-body-after-trauma — trauma can take away bodily autonomy, so healing often involves restoring choice; tattooing offers sensation a person chose, with a set duration, that can be paused or stopped; tattoos are "neither inherently pathological nor inherently therapeutic"; tattoos should never be prescribed as treatment; weigh medical safety, psychological readiness and informed consent.
- Weisenberger, C. (2025, May 20). "How To: Trauma-Informed Tattooing." Kingpin Tattoo Supply. https://www.kingpintattoosupply.com/blogs/stories-and-articles/how-to-trauma-informed-tattooing — artist practices: don't ask directly about trauma; let clients decide if, how and when to share; check in and remind clients they can pause, stop or speak up at any time; offer private options and expose only the area being tattooed; explain each action and ask before touching or moving the client; avoid edgy jokes and assumptions about boundaries.
- NPR (2025). "Ohio tattoo shop helps trauma and abuse survivors transform scars into art." https://www.npr.org/2025/04/29/nx-s1-5342586/ohio-tattoo-shop-helps-trauma-and-abuse-survivors-transform-scars-into-art — Phoenix Ink & Beauty (Amherst, Ohio), owner Angelique Agnew; "Art of Healing" program covers scars (self-harm, surgery, human trafficking survivors); participants pay only for ink and supplies; artists donate their time.
Present cover-up programs as examples found in reporting (programs change; check current details), never as SafeBAE partners.
