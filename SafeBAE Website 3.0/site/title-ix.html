Title IX Your rights at school

Title IX is a federal civil rights law that protects students from sex discrimination in schools and guarantees the right to an education free from harassment, assault, and inequity. Every student deserves to know these protections exist.

Is my school covered?→Supportive measures
Know your rights

So, what does it do?

Prohibits discrimination

Title IX prohibits sex-based discrimination in any school or educational program that receives federal funding.

Protects all students

Title IX protections apply to all students, regardless of gender identity or expression.

Covers various issues

Title IX covers a wide range of issues, including sexual harassment, assault, and other forms of gender-based violence.

70%

of students in SafeBAE’s national school climate survey said their school didn’t adequately teach their Title IX rights.

It is important to know your rights in order to protect yourself or a friend.

Take the 2-question quiz

Is my school covered by Title IX?

Do you attend a public or private school?

Here’s how to find out: Your school’s website likely lists this information under its “About Us” page or pages discussing its history, mission, or governance. You can also check your student handbook, enrollment forms, or ask a teacher or administrator.

Does your school receive any federal funding?

Here’s how to find out: Information about your school’s funding sources can likely be found in its annual reports, budgets, or financial statements. You can also check usaspending.gov or see if your school’s tax statements are available online. If you’re not able to find this information online, you can ask a school administrator, guidance counselor, or teacher.

Your school IS covered by Title IX

That means your school is legally required to prevent and respond to sex-based discrimination, including sexual harassment and assault, so every student has equal access to education.

Supportive measuresWho do I talk to?

Your school is NOT covered by Title IX

Private schools that receive no federal funding aren’t bound by Title IX. But your school may still have its own anti-discrimination and safety policies. Check your student handbook or ask an administrator about your rights and protections.

Push for a stronger policy

What Title IX laws really mean

Title IX addresses sexual violence and requires schools to proactively address, remedy, and eliminate sexual violence on their campuses. You are protected under Title IX regardless of your gender and how you identify.

  • Your school is required by law to ensure your safety on campus.
  • Your school is required to have established procedures for handling complaints of sexual discrimination, harassment, or violence.
  • You have the right to report sexual harassment or assault to your school, and your school MUST take steps to ensure victims do not have to share space, such as classes, with their abuser.
  • Your school should inform you of your right to counseling services and your right to make a report to law enforcement.
  • Your school may NOT retaliate against someone filing a complaint and must keep the complainant safe from retaliatory harassment.
  • Schools can NOT discourage you from continuing your education, and must provide reasonable accommodations for your safety.

Who do I talk to?

Go to your Title IX Coordinator

When in doubt, go directly to the school Title IX Director (sometimes called the Title IX Coordinator). Your school website should have that information, or you can ask the school secretary.

A school is in violation of Title IX if:

  • the survivor experiences harassment during the reporting process
  • the investigation isn’t prompt and impartial
  • the survivor faces retaliation
  • the school fails to provide reasonable accommodations or a no-contact order

Title IX laws are always changing, which is why knowing what is available to protect you is so crucial.

11 moments · 1972 to today

Timeline of Title IX

Select a year to read what changed.

1972

Title IX Enacted

Title IX of the Education Amendments of 1972 is signed into law, prohibiting sex-based discrimination in any education program or activity receiving federal financial assistance.

1977

Alexander v. Yale

A group of students filed a lawsuit against Yale University, arguing that the university’s failure to address sexual harassment constituted a violation of Title IX. This was the first case to use Title IX to argue that sexual harassment is a form of sex discrimination.

1992

Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools

This landmark decision marked the first successful interpretation of Title IX to cover sexual harassment, significantly strengthening the law’s enforcement and protections against gender-based discrimination in educational settings.

2011

The “Dear Colleague” Letter

The Obama administration’s Office for Civil Rights issues the “Dear Colleague” Letter, clarifying that sexual violence is a form of sexual harassment prohibited by Title IX and outlining schools’ responsibility to respond promptly and equitably to reports of sexual violence.

2017

Rescission of 2011 Guidance

The 2011 guidance is rescinded by the Trump/DeVos administration, and interim guidance is provided, allowing schools more flexibility in handling sexual violence cases and decreasing protections for survivors.

2020

The Trump/DeVos Title IX Rules

In May 2020, new Title IX regulations are published. These regulations require schools to follow specific procedures for investigating and adjudicating sexual harassment and assault cases, including live hearings and cross-examinations. These rules make it harder for survivors to get the support they need.

2022

Proposed Title IX Rule

After over a year of pressure from organizers, the Biden administration proposes new changes to the Title IX regulations on June 23, 2022 (the 50th anniversary of Title IX) to better protect students and ensure fair processes. These changes aimed to address some of the criticisms of the 2020 rules.

2024

Final Rule Update

New Title IX rules are finalized and released on April 19, 2024, and take effect August 1, 2024. These rules provided enhanced protections for students and survivors, including LGBTQ+ students and pregnant and postpartum students.

January 9, 2025

Vacatur and Post-Decision Status

A federal district court issues a decisive ruling that vacates the 2024 Final Rule in its entirety. The Department of Education has confirmed that the 2024 Title IX regulations and related resources are not effective in any jurisdiction, meaning the 2020 regulations are the rules schools must follow today.

June 16, 2026

Civil Rights Enforcement Moves to the DOJ

The U.S. Department of Education moves civil rights enforcement from OCR to the Department of Justice, shifting how harassment and discrimination complaints in K-12 schools and colleges are investigated and resolved, and making it even more confusing for students and families to understand where and how to seek justice.

Today

What Comes Next?

The future of Title IX and its enforcement at the federal level remains uncertain, and yet students still have the legal right to be protected in their schools.

Schools can do more

Since 2020, schools have been pulled back and forth between restrictive rules, attempts at expanded protections, and ongoing court challenges. This leaves survivors with fewer options, and schools without clear or consistent guidance. That’s why knowing about Title IX is important, but not enough on its own. Your school has the power to do better.

We created the School Policy Reform Guide to help students, educators, and administrators strengthen policies beyond the bare federal minimum. By adopting trauma-informed, survivor-centered practices, schools can ensure real safety, transparency, and accountability.
