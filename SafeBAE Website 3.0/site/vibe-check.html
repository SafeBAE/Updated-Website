No judgment. Just answers.

CheckYourVibe.org is a free, anonymous reflection tool for young people who are questioning whether they, or a friend, may have crossed a boundary or caused harm.

Did I harm someone? Was that consent? Did I cross a boundary? Am I a bad person? Not sure if you crossed a line?
Quick exit on every screen

Young people are taking hard questions about consent to AI chatbots and anonymous forums.

AI is designed to tell you what you want to hear. It doesn’t help you reflect or take accountability.

Vibe Check guides users through confusing situations and connects them with real, evidence-based resources on consent, communication, and accountability. It exists because many people have questions about situations that didn’t feel clear and want to do better, but aren’t sure where to start.

How it works

One step at a time, at your own pace

Vibe Check asks about a situation and how you’re feeling, then shows tools and information based on your answers. There are no right or wrong answers, no pressure to finish, and you can stop at any point.

The four parts of accountability

Vibe Check follows a framework named by Mia Mingus, drawn from transformative justice. Accountability has four parts, and they go in order.

1

Self-reflection

Look honestly at what you did and what came up for you, without minimizing, comparing, or rushing past it. This is the part of the work that happens before anyone else is involved.

2

Apologize

Name what you did specifically, center the other person, ask for nothing in return. Only if they want to hear it, and only after you’ve done part 1.

3

Repair

Do what you can to make things as right as possible: whatever they need, not what feels best to you. Sometimes repair is space. Sometimes it’s silence.

4

Change behavior

The load-bearing part. Preventing recurrence is the most meaningful thing anyone who’s caused harm can offer. Everything else is preparation for this.

Privacy

Nothing is stored.

No data is collected. Nothing is stored. It disappears when the browser closes. Vibe Check collects no names, no account info, and does not report anything to anyone.

What Vibe Check is

  • Completely privateNo accounts, no tracking, no history. Nothing is saved or sent anywhere.
  • Non-punitiveDesigned to build awareness and accountability, not to condemn, label, or shame.
  • Self-paced and self-guidedThere are no right or wrong answers, and no pressure to finish.
  • Practical next stepsInteractive activities, guided lessons, grounding exercises, and support resources.

What it isn’t

  • AI adviceIt connects you with real, evidence-based resources on consent, communication, and accountability.
  • Legal adviceIt doesn’t make legal determinations, determine guilt, or assign labels.
  • A replacement for professional supportIt is a self-guided reflection tool, not therapy, and not a crisis intervention.
  • Something to assignMaking it mandatory or punitive will guarantee it doesn’t work.

If a young person has been involved in a serious incident, experienced trauma, or is in crisis, please connect them with a qualified counselor or crisis service.

Check Their Vibe: an adults’ guide

Vibe Check is a free, private, self-guided reflection tool for young people navigating moments of sexual uncertainty. This guide helps you introduce it thoughtfully, follow up with care, and understand what it actually contains.

Download the guide (PDF) ↗Go through Vibe Check yourself ↗

“As the founding Executive Director of SafeBAE and a mom, this might be the most important tool we’ve developed.”

Shael Norris, SafeBAE
  1. Pick the right moment, not the charged one.Wait until things have cooled down. Good windows: a car ride, a casual one-on-one moment, a follow-up conversation after things have settled.
  2. Lead with curiosity, not direction.If it sounds like a punishment or an assignment, they won’t engage honestly, even if they do open it.
  3. Make the privacy real.Send them the link and step away. Don’t ask to see what they wrote or what path they took. Make it clear they can stop at any point.
  4. Tell them what it actually is.Young people are suspicious of anything that looks like a trap or feels fake. Being upfront about what the tool is, and isn’t, helps a lot.
  5. Give them genuine space, and keep the door open.Let them know you’re available to talk, but take the pressure off.

The perfect time is before anything has happened at all, as part of a talk about dating or sex.

Opening the conversation

“Did you get a chance to look at that thing I sent? No worries if not — just wondering what you thought.”

“Is there anything from it that stuck with you, or anything you want to talk about?”

“Did anything surprise you or feel off?”

If they seem uncomfortable

“You don’t have to tell me anything about it. I just wanted to check in that you’re okay.”

“If something came up that felt heavy, I want you to know you can talk to me — or someone else you trust.”

“Sometimes that stuff brings up more questions than answers. That’s fine — it’s actually a good sign.”

Going deeper (if they’re open)

“What does consent actually mean to you? What do you think it looks like in real life?”

“Have you ever been in a situation where you weren’t sure if someone was into it? What did you do?”

“How do you think you’d react if a friend told you they’d been in a situation where someone didn’t stop when asked?”

If they didn’t use it

“That’s okay — I’ll leave the link with you. It’s there whenever you want it.”

“Is there a reason it felt weird? I can explain what it actually is if that would help.”

“Even without the tool — is there anything on your mind about what we talked about?”

If a young person comes to you after using it, receive that with curiosity, not judgment. Your reaction to the first vulnerable thing they share determines whether they share anything again.

What you might noticeWhat it might mean, and how to respond
They bring it up unpromptedThey engaged with it. Stay curious and follow their lead. Don’t pepper them with questions.
They’re quieter than usual, seem to be processing somethingGive it time before asking. Something may have landed. Let them sit with it.
They dismiss it or say it was “stupid” or “not for them”They may have engaged but felt defensive. Don’t push. The seed is planted.
They say they didn’t do itDon’t make it a confrontation. Leave the link available, and come back differently another time.
They ask questions about consent, relationships, or something relatedThey’re processing. This is success. Engage with their question directly and honestly.
They become upset or shut down during the follow-up conversationBack off. Let them know the door is open when they’re ready. Don’t force the conversation.
Watching them use it

Your physical presence changes how honest someone is. Give them the link, explain what it is, and leave.

Treating it as evidence or confession

This tool is not a mechanism for extracting admissions. Using anything from it against them destroys the safety the tool requires.

Introducing it in a charged moment

A young person who is defensive, frightened, or angry cannot engage with this tool honestly.

Announcing it publicly or to their peer group

If other people know a young person is being referred to this tool, shame and humiliation take over. Keep it between you and them.

Following up like an interrogation

“What did you learn?” or “Did you understand what you did wrong?” puts a young person on the defensive. The goal is a conversation, not a report-back.

Is this appropriate for my child specifically?

Vibe Check works best with young people who are at least 13–14 years old and have some emotional readiness to reflect. It is not designed as a crisis intervention and should not be the first or only response to a serious disclosure. Consider whether a counselor should be involved first.

What if they’ve actually done something harmful to someone?

The tool is designed for young people who are in or near the grey zone of harmful behavior. It approaches accountability without shame spiraling. If the behavior is serious, involving assault, coercion, minors, or legal exposure, it is not a substitute for professional intervention, counseling, or legal process.

Can I go through the tool myself first?

Yes, and you should. It helps you understand the tone, what the questions feel like, and what resources get offered. You can explore it as someone reflecting on a scenario, or skip ahead to the Next Steps page to see the resources, tools, and modules directly.

What if they refuse to use it?

Don’t force it. Making this mandatory or punitive will guarantee it doesn’t work. Leave the link somewhere available and return to it casually later. The conversation you’re trying to open doesn’t have to start with the tool.

Does this replace a conversation with a therapist or counselor?

No. This is a self-guided reflection tool, not therapy. It can be a useful complement to counseling or a first step toward self-awareness. It is not clinical support.

What if something comes up that I don’t know how to handle?

“I don’t know the answer to that, but I can find out” is one of the most trust-building things you can say. If a young person discloses something serious, stay calm, validate that they told you, and help them access the right support. The Support section of the tool includes crisis resources.

Accountability is relational.
Dominick Shattuck, PhD, on Vibe Check
DS
Dominick Shattuck, PhDNote on Substack
Read on Substack ↗

Where are young people learning how to think about relationships, consent, accountability, and repair?

Increasingly, many are turning to AI tools, Reddit forums, influencers, or online communities that frame these experiences through blame, defensiveness, misogyny, punishment, or emotional isolation. That concerns me deeply, especially for young men who are already struggling with loneliness, confusion, and a lack of healthy relational guidance.

I recently came across Vibe Check, a new project from SafeBAE, and I think it represents something genuinely important.

Vibe Check is a free and fully anonymous reflection tool designed for young people who are questioning whether they or a friend may have crossed a boundary, caused harm, or handled a situation poorly.

Instead of escalating shame or encouraging denial, the tool walks users through reflection, accountability, empathy, communication, and repair. Importantly, it is intentionally not AI-generated. It was built from years of direct work with young people around consent, relationships, and sexual violence prevention.

What I appreciate most is that it recognizes something many conversations miss: Accountability is relational.

People rarely grow through humiliation alone. Growth often happens when individuals are given structured opportunities to reflect honestly, understand impact, reconnect to others, and move toward repair.

That does not minimize harm. It creates conditions where accountability is more likely to happen.

At a time when so many young people are searching online for answers about deeply human situations, I think tools like this matter.
