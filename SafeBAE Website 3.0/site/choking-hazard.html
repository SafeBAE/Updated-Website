A SafeBAE campaign · chokinghazard.org

There’s no safe way to choke someone.

Prevention has to start early, before the behavior does.

Choking Hazard is SafeBAE’s campaign on sexual strangulation, which young people call “choking.” By college, many young people have already heard about choking or encountered it in sexual contexts. Waiting until after high school to talk about the impact and harm of sexual strangulation is too late.

National U.S. survey · 2022

It starts before college.

In a 2022 national U.S. survey, 11.9% of sexually experienced girls aged 14 to 17 said a partner had choked them. Among women aged 18 to 24 it was 31.9%, and among women aged 25 to 29, 40.6%.

15

is the average age students first heard about choking during sex.

Where they first heard about it

Friends35%
Pornography34.5%

Herbenick et al., 2025. Bars on a 0–100% scale.

1 in 4 college students who had been choked were first choked between ages 10 and 17.

Herbenick et al., 2022

Less force than opening a can.

The carotid arteries carry blood to the brain. They can close under about 11 pounds of force. Pulling open the tab on a can takes about 20.

Armstrong & Strack, 2016

These are not safe limits. There is no safe amount of force.

Seconds matter.

A person can pass out 5 to 10 seconds after the carotid arteries close. In one study the average was 6.8 seconds. Breathing can stop by about 62 seconds.

Myth vs. fact

What you’ve heard. What’s actually true.

What you’ve heardWhat’s actually true

“It’s just choking.”Myth

It’s strangulation.

“Choking” is the word people use. What happens is strangulation: pressure on the neck that cuts off blood to the brain.

In abusive relationships it is one of the strongest warning signs that abuse will turn deadly. Women strangled by an abusive partner had 7.48 times the odds of later being killed by that partner.

Glass et al., 2008

“It’s safe if you do it right.”Myth

There’s no safe way.

The carotid arteries can close under about 11 pounds of force, less than the 20 it takes to open a can, and a person can pass out within 5 to 10 seconds. These are not safe limits.

Armstrong & Strack, 2016; Rossen et al., 1943

“No marks, no harm.”Myth

No marks means no marks.

In a review of 300 cases, half had no visible neck injury and 35% had marks too small to photograph.

  • 150 no visible neck injury
  • 105 marks too small to photograph
  • 45 injuries visible enough to photograph

Strack, McClane & Hawley, 2001

“They said yes, so it’s consent.”Myth

You’re still responsible for the harm.

Strangulation is a crime in every US state. All 50 states, Washington, D.C., and three US territories have added strangulation or suffocation to their criminal codes, most of them under aggravated assault. If a partner is accidentally hurt or killed, the person who choked them may be held legally and criminally responsible, even when both agreed to it. Laws differ from state to state.

Alliance for HOPE International, 2026

“They felt fine after, so they’re fine.”Myth

Some of it shows up later.

Swelling in the throat can develop hours later. A stroke from a torn artery can happen days or weeks later. If symptoms start or get worse, go back for care, even if you were already seen. Signs that need the ER

Training Institute on Strangulation Prevention, rev. 2022

Each fact, with the research behind it, is on chokinghazard.org.

Consent

The person doing it is more likely to think it was consensual.

A 2025 survey of 4,702 Australians aged 18 to 35 asked people who had choked a partner, and people who had been choked, how consent was given the last time it happened.

1 in 2

U.S. college students choked during their most recent sex had no agreed word or signal to stop.

38%

relied on body language alone.

Herbenick et al., 2025

If you’re the one choking someone, don’t assume they agreed.

Choking can’t meet the pillars of consent.

Even if two people agree to it beforehand, the pillars of consent can’t be met once the choking starts.

FailsS

Sober

Consent needs a clear head.

Strangulation cuts off blood to the brain. The person being choked stops thinking clearly before they pass out.

FailsR

Revocable

Consent means you can stop it at any time.

In experiments, some people being strangled could not speak or move to say stop, and some forgot they could end it.

FailsO

Ongoing

Consent has to last the whole time.

A person can pass out 5 to 10 seconds after the carotid arteries close. Someone who is unconscious can’t agree to anything.

FailsE

Enthusiastic

Consent has to be clear and keep being shown.

You can’t tell by looking whether someone is enjoying it or passing out.

These are four of SafeBAE’s six pillars of consent, MOVERS: Mutual, Ongoing, Voluntary, Enthusiastic, Revocable and Sober. The film It’s Giving Red Flags teaches all six.

Where it came from · six exhibits

It didn’t come out of nowhere.

It’s in your feed, sold as merch, shelved as romance, shared as a meme, written as fan fiction, and even on the Billboard Hot 100.

How screens and books show choking

SafeBAE rates TV, film, comedy and bestselling books that show choking. Scenes that play it as comedy, eroticize it, romanticize it or treat it as harmless are rated CH. Work that shows the harm or treats it as dangerous is rated BAE.

If it has already happened

Get medical help. Say what happened.

Some injuries show up later. Swelling in the throat can develop hours afterward, and a stroke from a torn artery can happen days or weeks later.

Go to an emergency room today if:

Even with no marks, and even if you agreed to it.

  • You passed out, even briefly, or can’t remember part of what happened
  • Your breathing has changed
  • Swallowing hurts or is difficult
  • Your voice is hoarse, raspy or deeper, or it’s hard to speak
  • Your neck hurts or is tender, or there’s a mark or bruise on it
  • Your vision or hearing has changed, or your ears are ringing
  • You have a headache, or feel dizzy or lightheaded
  • There are small red spots in your eyes or on your face
  • You lost control of your bladder or bowels

Call 911

if someone:

  • is unconscious
  • has trouble breathing
  • has a swelling neck
  • seems confused
  • has weakness or numbness on one side
  • has a seizure
  • shows signs of a stroke: a drooping face, a weak arm, slurred speech

If symptoms start or get worse later, go back, even if you were already seen. A drooping face, a weak arm or slurred speech at any time in the weeks after means call 911.

At the front desk, say

“I was strangled.”

Not only “my throat hurts.” The word tells staff which injuries to check for.

If you’re under 18

You can still get medical care. Staff may be required to report abuse of a minor, and you can ask what they would report before you share more.

Training Institute on Strangulation Prevention medical recommendations (Smock, Green & Sturgeon, rev. 2022); Institute for Addressing Strangulation (2024); Faculty of Forensic & Legal Medicine (UK, 2023)

What to expect at the ER ↗

If a friend tells you it happened

What you say first affects whether your friend tells anyone else and whether they get medical care.

Say

  • “I’m really sorry that happened to you.”
  • “Are you hurt? Do you want me to go with you to get checked?”
  • “What can I do to help?”

Don’t say

  • “Why didn’t you stop it?”
  • “Lots of people are into that.”
  • “You seem fine, so it probably wasn’t a big deal.”

If your friend has any of the warning signs, go with them to an emergency room today. If they have trouble breathing, seem confused or pass out, call 911.
