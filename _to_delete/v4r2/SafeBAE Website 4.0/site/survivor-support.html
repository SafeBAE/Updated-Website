From Choking Hazard, SafeBAE’s campaign on sexual strangulation

If you were choked or strangled

Go to an ER today, even with no marks and even if you agreed to it, if you:

  • Passed out, even briefly, or can’t remember part of what happened
  • Breathing changes, or pain or difficulty swallowing
  • A hoarse, raspy or deeper voice, or trouble speaking
  • Neck pain or tenderness, or a mark or bruise on your neck
  • Vision changes, ringing ears, or hearing changes
  • A headache, dizziness or lightheadedness
  • Small red spots in your eyes or on your face
  • Lost bladder or bowel control

At the front desk, say “I was strangled.” Not just “my throat hurts.”

Call 911 if someone is unconscious, has trouble breathing, has a swelling neck, seems confused, has one-sided weakness or numbness, has a seizure, or shows stroke signs. Symptoms can show up later: throat swelling can develop hours later, and a torn-artery stroke can occur days or weeks later.

What to know about choking→

Healing takes time, and support.

Connection with peers, supportive communities, or professionals can ease feelings of isolation, reduce guilt or shame, and provide tools for coping. Even when survivors understand they are not to blame, it often takes time and support to fully believe it.

For some, reconnecting with their body and emotions may feel overwhelming. Some survivors experience disconnection or turn to risky behaviors to manage pain. While facing these feelings can be difficult, avoiding them can cause further harm. With compassionate support and mental health resources, survivors can begin to process trauma, reclaim safety, and move toward healing.

Dating after sexual assault

Relationships and intimacy after assault can bring new challenges. Survivors may face triggers that complicate dating, while partners may feel uncertain about how to provide support. To open space for these conversations, SafeBAE partnered with Teen Vogue to produce Dating After Sexual Assault.

When someone tells you they’ve been assaulted

Opening up about sexual violence takes tremendous courage. If someone chooses to share their experience with you, here’s how to show up for them.

How to support a friend (FAQ)

Start by believing.

The single best way to support a survivor is to believe them. Questioning their account, even out of shock, can be devastating and may keep them from ever opening up again.

Listen more than you speak.

Show you’re engaged: make eye contact, don’t interrupt, and let them share as much or as little as they want. You don’t need details to be helpful. Never press for them.

Validate their feelings.

If they minimize what happened (“other people have it worse”), gently remind them they’re allowed to be as upset as they need to be. No assault is “not bad enough” to matter.

Meet immediate needs first.

Before processing emotions, make sure they feel physically safe and cared for. Something as simple as a glass of water, a snack, a cozy blanket, or asking if they want a hug can signal to their brain that they’re safe now.

Let them lead.

Follow their choices about whether to report, who to tell, and how to cope. Reporting can be re-traumatizing, so never pressure them to. You can help them understand their options while respecting whatever they decide.

Words that help, and words that hurt

Try saying

Words that help

  • “I’m so sorry this happened to you.”
  • “You are not alone. I know there are people that can help.”
  • “How can I support you?”
  • “It’s not your fault.”
  • “I’m glad you felt safe to tell me so I can be here for you.”
  • “You didn’t deserve that to happen to you.”
Don’t say

Words to avoid

  • “Why didn’t you just leave?”
  • “Why were you there?”
  • “If you kissed, you should have known something was going to happen.”
  • “Were you drinking?”
  • “What were you wearing?”
  • “It’s going to be fine; just move on.”
  • “If it was really a big deal, why didn’t you tell me sooner?”
