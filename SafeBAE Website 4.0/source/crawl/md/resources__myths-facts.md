# Let’s debunk the myths about sexual assault.

We want to debunk the myths surrounding sexual assault. Below, we break down the most common myths about sexual violence, look at what the research actually says, and dig into who is most affected — and why so much of this goes unreported.

## What causes sexual violence?

People blame a lot of things — being under the influence, the clothes someone is wearing, walking alone, flirty behavior, or hooking up with a lot of people. None of these cause sexual violence. It happens when someone chooses to ignore or violate another person’s boundaries and consent.

That choice is often reinforced by rape culture — victim-blaming, harmful stereotypes, peer pressure, media, algorithms, and beliefs about gender, sexuality, power, and entitlement. Prevention means holding people accountable and challenging the culture that excuses harm.

## Why don't survivors report?

If a survivor doesn’t report to the police, it does NOT mean the violence didn’t happen. There are many reasons someone might not come forward:

* It’s emotionally painful, and trauma can make details hard to remember.
* Shame and stigma — fear of being judged, blamed, or “slut-shamed.”
* Fear of not being believed, or that people will defend the perpetrator.
* Fear that nothing will be done — fewer than 2% of reports lead to a conviction.
* Fear of punishment or retaliation, especially for teens.
* Not realizing what happened was abuse in the first place.

## Real Example: Megan Thee Stallion

In 2020, Megan Thee Stallion was harmed by someone she knew. When police arrived, she said she had stepped on glass because she feared the situation could escalate into police violence. After she later spoke out, she was mocked, doubted, and blamed publicly.

Her story shows why reporting is never simple — and why we should never assume silence means nothing happened. Survivors deserve to be believed, supported, and given choices.

# **Myths vs. Facts**

Myths vs. Facts: Sexual Violence

### Masculinity & Male Survivors

Myth

Men are too strong to be assaulted.

Reveal the fact →

Fact

Strength does not prevent sexual violence. At least **1 in 6 men** will experience sexual abuse or assault in their lifetime.

Myth

Men always want sex and welcome all sexual advances.

Reveal the fact →

Fact

Sexual assault is never wanted. It is a traumatic experience for any survivor, regardless of gender.

Myth

Only gay men commit sexual violence against other men.

Reveal the fact →

Fact

Sexual violence is about **power and control**, not sexual orientation. Survivors and perpetrators can be of any gender or identity.

Myth

Men abused by women “got lucky.”

Reveal the fact →

Fact

Sexual violence is never a privilege. Women can—and do—sexually abuse or assault boys and men. Survivors are not “lucky”; they are harmed and exploited.

### Who It Affects & Who Commits It

Myth

“Real rape” is committed by strangers.

Reveal the fact →

Fact

Anyone can be a perpetrator—date rape and stranger rape are both rape. Most rapes are committed by someone the victim knows: **92% of female victims** and **74% of male victims** report perpetrators who were acquaintances or intimate partners, and as many as **93% of child sexual abuse survivors** knew their abuser.

Myth

Only women can be raped.

Reveal the fact →

Fact

Sexual assault is experienced by people of all genders. **1 out of 10** rape victims is male, and **1 in 2** transgender people are sexually assaulted in their lifetime.

Myth

Sexual assault can’t happen within a marriage or relationship.

Reveal the fact →

Fact

Consent is required every time, in every relationship. A relationship—or a past “yes”—is never standing consent. Intimate partner sexual violence is real, harmful, and a crime in all 50 states.

Myth

Sexual violence doesn’t happen to people with disabilities.

Reveal the fact →

Fact

People with disabilities experience sexual violence at far higher rates. People with intellectual disabilities are sexually assaulted at **7 times the rate** of people without disabilities—and their abusers are often people they know and rely on.

### Blame & Believing Survivors

Myth

False claims of rape are common—most are made for revenge, regret, or attention.

Reveal the fact →

Fact

False reporting rates for sexual assault are in the **2–10% range**—the same as other crimes. The far bigger problem is under-reporting: the majority of sexual assaults are never reported to authorities at all.

Myth

Clothing choices, location, and drinking can prevent rape and harassment.

Reveal the fact →

Fact

Sexual assault is about **power, not clothing**. This logic holds survivors responsible for their own assaults instead of placing responsibility where it belongs: on perpetrators.

Myth

If they didn’t fight back, it wasn’t really assault.

Reveal the fact →

Fact

Freezing is one of the most common trauma responses. Many survivors experience **tonic immobility**—an involuntary reaction that makes it impossible to move or speak. Not fighting back is never consent.

Myth

If it really happened, they would have reported it right away.

Reveal the fact →

Fact

Delayed reporting is the norm, not the exception. Fear, shame, self-blame, and trauma keep many survivors silent for months, years, or forever. A delay says nothing about the truth of what happened.

## Who is most at risk?

Different identities — and the intersections between them — play a big role in the risk of experiencing violence and in access to reporting and healing resources. As you read these statistics, remember that there’s a lack of research on many marginalized groups, so these numbers are subject to change, and this is in no way a comprehensive list.
